Finding customers

How to get your first 10 customers (without being pushy)

Many people starting a business later in life dread the "selling" part. The good news is that your first customers rarely come from hard selling. They come from telling people clearly what you do, making it easy to say yes, and doing great work that people talk about.

In short

  • Your first customers will most likely come from people who already know and trust you.
  • A clear one-sentence description of what you do makes it easy for others to refer you.
  • Show up where local customers look: Google, a simple website, and one or two community places.
  • Ask every happy customer for a review and a referral. It is the single most effective habit.

Get clear on who you help first

It is hard to find customers if you have not decided who they are. "Anyone who needs it" makes your message vague and forgettable. Before you tell anyone about your business, finish this sentence:

"I help [specific type of customer] with [specific problem] so they can [result they want]."

Some examples:

  • "I help busy families keep their gardens tidy all year, without them lifting a finger."
  • "I help small cafes and restaurants keep their books in order, so tax time is stress-free."
  • "I help people in their 60s downsize to a smaller home, from sorting belongings to moving day."

This one sentence is the foundation of everything below. It is what you will say in conversations, write in messages and put at the top of your website. It also makes it easy for other people to refer you, because they know exactly who to send your way.

Start with the people who already know you

Your first customers will very likely be people you know, or people they know. This is not cheating, and it is not "using" your friends. It is how most small businesses begin.

Make your list

Write down everyone who might be a customer or might know one: friends, family, neighbours, former colleagues and managers, people from clubs, church, school gates, sports teams, past suppliers and clients. Most people over 40 can list a hundred or more names in an evening.

Send personal messages, not a broadcast

A personal message gets far more response than a social media announcement. Keep it short, warm and specific:

"Hi Sarah, I hope you're well. After 20 years at the council I've started my own business helping families sort out and downsize their homes. If you know anyone who's moving, or helping a parent move, I'd really appreciate it if you passed my name on. Here's my website if it's useful: [link]. Thanks so much!"

Notice what this does: it explains the change, says exactly who you help, and asks for a referral rather than a sale. Nobody feels pressured, and many people genuinely like helping someone they know.

Make it easy to say yes

When someone is interested, remove every obstacle between them and a booking:

  • Clear prices, or at least a "from" price. People who cannot see a price often assume it is too high and leave. See how to price your services.
  • One simple next step: "Book a free 15-minute call", "Text me for a quote", "Order by Thursday for Saturday".
  • Quick replies. Responding within a few hours wins jobs. Many customers contact two or three businesses and go with whoever answers first.
  • An introductory offer, if it suits your business: a first-session discount, a free assessment, or a small package to try.
  • Easy payment: card, bank transfer or a payment link, not just cash. Be wary of a new "customer" who overpays and asks for the difference back; it is one of several scams that target new small businesses.

Be easy to find locally

After a referral, almost everyone searches for you online before getting in touch. And people who do not know you yet search for things like "dog walker near me" or "bookkeeper in [town]". You need to show up for both.

  1. Set up a free Google Business Profile. This is how you appear on Google Maps and in local search results. It is one of the most powerful free tools for any local business. Follow our step-by-step guide.
  2. Have a simple website. It confirms you are real and professional, answers common questions and shows your prices and contact details. Our guide on whether you need a website if you're on Instagram explains why it still matters.
  3. List yourself in a few relevant directories such as local chamber of commerce listings, industry associations or trusted local directories.
  4. Keep details consistent. Use exactly the same business name, phone number and address everywhere.

Show up in your community

Local businesses grow through being seen and known. A few low-cost ideas:

  • Local online groups. Neighbourhood Facebook groups and community forums often allow business posts on certain days, and members regularly ask for recommendations. Be helpful first, not salesy.
  • Partner with related businesses. A dog walker and a vet, a bookkeeper and an accountant, a home organiser and an estate agent. Refer customers to each other.
  • Give a free talk. Libraries, clubs, community centres and business groups often welcome speakers. "Five things to do before you downsize" positions you as the expert.
  • Markets, fairs and events for businesses that sell products or services you can show.
  • Old-fashioned flyers and noticeboards still work for very local services, especially for older customers.

Turn every happy customer into more customers

Once you have a few customers, the most powerful growth comes from them. Build two habits from your very first job.

Ask for a review

Reviews, especially on Google, help strangers trust you and help you appear higher in local search. Ask right after a job goes well, and send a direct link so it takes them seconds:

"It was a pleasure helping you this week. If you have a minute, a short Google review would mean a lot to a small business like mine. Here's the link: [link]. Thank you!"

Reply to every review, good or bad, politely. Never pay for reviews or post fake ones; it breaks the rules of review platforms and can get your profile penalised. For more wording, and how to handle a bad review, see how to get your first customer reviews.

Ask for a referral

People rarely refer unless asked. A simple line at the end of a job works: "If you know anyone else who could use this, I'd be very grateful for an introduction." Some businesses offer a small thank-you, such as a discount on the next booking, for each referral.

Follow up, politely

Most people who are interested do not book the first time. They get busy and forget. A single friendly follow-up wins a surprising amount of work:

  • After sending a quote, check in after a few days: "Just checking you received my quote. Happy to answer any questions."
  • After a job, send a thank-you and a reminder of when they might need you next.
  • Keep a simple list of past customers and get in touch at sensible times: seasonal reminders, new services, or a short newsletter.

Following up is not pushy. It is good service.

A simple 30-day plan

A 30-day plan for getting first customers
WeekFocusActions
1Get readyWrite your one-sentence description, set prices, set up a Google Business Profile and a simple web page
2Tell your networkSend 30 personal messages; ask for referrals, not sales
3Show up locallyPost helpfully in two local groups; approach two partner businesses
4Follow up and askFollow up every lead; ask every customer for a review and a referral

Repeat weeks two to four every month. Within a few months, referrals and reviews start bringing customers to you, and the "selling" becomes simply answering the phone.

Common questions

Where do most small businesses find their first customers?

Usually through personal networks: friends, former colleagues, neighbours, and referrals from them. Local search on Google, community groups and word of mouth usually follow once the business has a few happy customers and reviews.

Should I offer free work to get started?

It is usually better to offer a fair introductory price than to work for free. Paid work tells you whether there is real demand, and people tend to value what they pay for. A free sample, consultation or short trial can work well if it leads naturally to paid work.

Do I need social media to get customers?

Not necessarily. Many local businesses get most customers from Google searches, referrals and repeat business. If you use social media, choose one platform your customers actually use and post regularly, rather than spreading yourself thin.

How do I ask for reviews without feeling awkward?

Ask right after a job goes well, when the customer is happiest, and make it easy by sending a direct link. Something like: "It was a pleasure working with you. If you have a minute, a short Google review would really help a small business like mine."
