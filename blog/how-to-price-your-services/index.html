Finding customers

How to price your services when you're just starting out

Pricing is where many new owners lose money without noticing. Charge too little and you work hard for nothing, attract the wrong customers and burn out. This guide gives you a simple, step-by-step way to set prices you can defend, and the confidence to raise them.

In short

  • Work out your minimum hourly rate from your real costs and the hours you can actually bill.
  • Check what others charge, then position yourself on value, not as the cheapest option.
  • Packages and fixed prices are easier for customers to say yes to than open-ended hourly rates.
  • Review prices at least once a year, and raise them when you are consistently busy.

Why new owners undercharge

Almost everyone undercharges at first. The reasons are understandable: you are nervous about whether anyone will pay, you compare yourself to big companies, or you price from what you would be willing to pay. People who come from employment often also forget that a business has costs a job never did.

When you were employed, your employer paid for your holidays, sick days, pension contributions, equipment, training, insurance, office and the time you spent in meetings and on admin. Now you pay for all of that, out of your prices.

Step 1: Work out your minimum rate

Your minimum rate is the lowest price at which the business is worth running. You should not normally go below it. Here is a simple way to calculate it for a service business.

Add up what you need per year

  • Your target income before tax: what you want to pay yourself.
  • Business costs: insurance, website, phone, software, travel, equipment, training, accountant, marketing. Our guide to start-up and running costs lists the ones that are easy to miss.
  • Taxes and contributions you will now pay yourself, which vary by country.
  • Retirement savings and health cover that an employer used to provide, if relevant.

Count your real billable hours

This is the step people skip. You will not bill 40 hours a week. Take away holidays, public holidays and sick days, then remember that much of each week goes on unpaid work: answering enquiries, quotes, admin, travel, accounts and marketing. Many self-employed people find they can bill only half to two-thirds of their working time.

Divide

Minimum hourly rate = total yearly amount needed ÷ billable hours per year.

Worked example of calculating a minimum hourly rate
ItemExample figure
Target income$50,000
Business costs$6,000
Extra taxes, retirement and cover (rough)$14,000
Total needed per year$70,000
Working weeks (after holidays and sick days)44
Billable hours per week22
Billable hours per year968
Minimum hourly rateAbout $72 an hour

These are example figures only, to show the method. Many people are shocked that the answer is far above their old hourly wage. That is normal, and it is why so many new businesses struggle when they price like employees.

Part-time or side business?

Do the same calculation with your real numbers: the extra income you want, the costs, and the hours you actually have. Even for a small side business, knowing your minimum stops you working for less than it costs you.

Step 2: Check the market

Your minimum rate tells you what you need. The market tells you what customers expect. Research what similar businesses in your area, or online, charge:

  • Look at competitors' websites and price lists.
  • Ask friends who have bought similar services what they paid.
  • Check industry associations, which sometimes publish typical rates.
  • Ask a few potential customers what they currently pay, or have paid before.

If the market rate is well above your minimum, good: you have room. If it is below your minimum, you need to either offer something clearly more valuable, find a different type of customer, or reconsider the idea.

Step 3: Decide where you sit

You do not have to be the cheapest. In fact, it is usually a bad position for a small business: big firms can always undercut you, and price-focused customers leave as soon as someone cheaper appears.

Instead, decide what makes you worth choosing. For many people over 40 the answer is experience and reliability:

  • "Thirty years in the trade, and I turn up when I say I will."
  • "A former head teacher who knows exactly what exam markers look for."
  • "Qualified accountant, with a fixed monthly fee and no surprise bills."

Customers who value these things will happily pay a mid-range or premium price.

Step 4: Choose how to charge

The power of three options

Offering three packages works well because customers compare options with each other instead of deciding yes or no. Many choose the middle option. For example, a garden service might offer a one-off tidy, a monthly visit and a full seasonal plan.

Step 5: Present your prices with confidence

  • Show prices or a "from" price on your website. It builds trust and filters out people who are not a fit.
  • Explain what is included, so customers compare value, not just numbers.
  • Say your price plainly and stop talking. Do not apologise or immediately offer a discount.
  • Put quotes in writing with what is included, the price, payment terms and how long the quote is valid.
  • Ask for a deposit on larger jobs, which reduces no-shows and protects your cash flow.

Step 6: Discounts, friends and family

Discounts are not wrong, but use them on purpose:

  • Introductory offers should have an end date and be clearly labelled.
  • Discount for commitment, not for asking. A lower monthly price for a 6-month booking makes sense; a lower price because someone haggled does not.
  • Friends and family: decide your policy in advance. Many owners give a small discount or nothing at all. Real friends want your business to succeed.

Step 7: Raise prices regularly

Costs rise every year, and so does your skill. Review your prices at least once a year. Clear signs that you should raise them:

  • You are fully booked or turning work away.
  • Nobody ever questions your price.
  • You are working long hours but profits are thin.
  • Your costs have gone up.

Give existing customers notice, explain briefly, and thank them. New customers simply see the new price. Most good customers stay, and if a few price-focused ones leave, you free up time for better-paying work.

Good pricing is not greedy. It is what lets you keep doing good work, for the long term. When your prices are set, make sure people can see them: our guide to getting your first customers covers the next step.

Common questions

Should I charge by the hour or by the project?

Hourly pricing is simple and suits work where the time is hard to predict. Project or package pricing is often better for both sides when the work is well defined: customers know the cost upfront, and you earn more as you get faster.

Is it better to be the cheapest to win customers?

Rarely. The cheapest option attracts price-sensitive customers who often leave as soon as someone cheaper appears, and it leaves you no margin for mistakes or growth. Aim to be clearly worth the price instead.

Should I show prices on my website?

For most small service businesses, showing at least a starting price helps. It filters out people who cannot afford you, builds trust and saves time on enquiries. Complex, custom work can use "from" prices or ranges.

How do I tell existing customers about a price increase?

Give notice, usually 30 days or more, explain briefly and positively, and thank them for their business. Most customers accept reasonable increases, especially if your service is good. You can keep loyal customers on the old price for a short period if you wish.
