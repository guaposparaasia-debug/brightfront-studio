How to take card payments in your small business, step by step

If you have only taken cash so far, card payments can feel like one more thing to learn. The good news is that it is much simpler than it used to be. Many small businesses start with a small reader and a phone. This guide explains your choices in plain words, what they cost, and how to set up safely.

Why card payments matter, even for a small business

Many people now carry little or no cash. They pay for almost everything with a bank card or their phone. If you only take cash, some customers will simply walk away, or promise to "come back later" and never do.

Taking cards also helps you in other ways:

  • Fewer lost sales. People can buy on the spot, even if they have no cash on them.
  • Less cash to carry and count. Fewer trips to the bank, and less worry about keeping cash safe.
  • Simple records. Most systems keep a list of every sale for you, which helps at tax time.

You do not have to stop taking cash. Most small businesses take both. The aim is simply to make it easy for every customer to pay you.

The three main ways to take card payments

There are three common ways to take card payments in a small business. You may only need one.

1. A small card reader linked to your phone

This is a small device, about the size of a coaster, that talks to an app on your phone or tablet. The customer taps or inserts their card, or holds their phone near it. The app shows you the sale and can send the customer a receipt by text or email.

It is light, cheap to start, and works almost anywhere there is a phone signal or Wi-Fi. That makes it a popular first choice for market stalls, home businesses and people who visit customers.

2. A counter till system

This is a fuller set-up for a shop or café counter. It usually includes a screen, a card reader, and sometimes a cash drawer and receipt printer. Some can also track your stock and staff hours.

It costs more to set up, but it can save time if you have a steady flow of customers at a counter every day.

3. A payment link or online invoice

Here you send the customer a link by text or email. They click it and pay by card on their own phone or computer. No reader is needed.

This suits work done in a customer's home, deposits for bookings, or anything you sell online or by phone. It is also a handy backup if your reader stops working.

What it costs, in plain words

Card payments are not free, but for most small businesses the cost is small compared with the sales you might otherwise lose. There are usually up to three kinds of cost:

  • The card reader. A one-off cost to buy the device. Simple readers are usually inexpensive. Till systems cost more.
  • A transaction fee. This is a small cut the payment company keeps from each card sale. It is usually a percentage of the sale, sometimes plus a few pence or cents. For many simple reader services it is often somewhere around 1 to 3 percent, but it varies by company, country and type of card.
  • A monthly fee. Some services charge a fixed amount each month, often for extra features. Many simple reader services charge no monthly fee.

When you compare options, look at the total cost for how you actually sell. A service with no monthly fee is often best when you are starting out and sales are small. Always check each company's current price list yourself before you sign up, as prices change.

Build the fee into your prices

The transaction fee is a normal cost of doing business, like your rent or materials. Include it when you work out your prices. Our guide on how to price your services shows how. Our breakdown of start-up costs helps you add up the reader and other set-up costs.

Setting up: a simple checklist

  1. Choose a payment company. Look at two or three well-known providers. Compare the reader price, the transaction fee, any monthly fee, and how fast they pay you.
  2. Sign up online. You will usually need your name, address, business details and bank account. Some will ask for proof of identity.
  3. Use a separate business bank account if you can. It keeps your business money apart and makes tax time much easier.
  4. Order the reader and download the provider's app onto your phone or tablet.
  5. Pair the reader with your phone. The app walks you through this, usually by Bluetooth, which is a short-range wireless link.
  6. Add your products or services and prices to the app, so taking payment is quick.
  7. Do a test sale. Charge yourself a small amount, then refund it, so you know how it works before a real customer is waiting.
  8. Put up a sign that says "Card and cash welcome", so customers know they can pay either way.

How and when the money reaches your bank

When a customer pays by card, the money does not land in your bank straight away. The payment company collects it, takes its fee, and then pays the rest into your bank account. This is called a payout.

  • Timing: many providers pay out within one to a few working days. Some offer faster payouts for an extra fee.
  • Check the app: it will show each sale, the fee taken, and when each payout is due.
  • Keep the records: download or save your monthly sales summaries. Your accountant will want them.

Plan for this short delay, especially in your first weeks, so you are not caught short when bills are due.

Phone and watch payments

Many customers now pay by holding their phone or watch near the reader, using Apple Pay or Google Pay. This is called a contactless payment, which simply means paying by tapping instead of inserting a card.

The good news is that you usually do not need anything extra. Most modern card readers accept contactless cards, phones and watches in the same way. When you choose a reader, just check that it says it takes contactless payments.

Staying safe

Card payments are safe when you follow a few simple rules:

  • Never write down a customer's card number, and never ask for it by text or email. Let the reader or payment link handle it.
  • Never take card details over the phone and type them in yourself unless your provider has a proper, secure way to do it.
  • Watch for fake payment screens. A customer showing you a "payment sent" screen on their own phone is not proof of payment. Only trust what appears in your own app.
  • Be wary of overpayments. If someone overpays and asks for the difference back, it is often a scam.
  • Keep your phone locked with a PIN or fingerprint, and turn on two-step login for your payment account.
  • Only use the provider's official app, downloaded from your phone's official app store.

Your backup plan if the reader or Wi-Fi stops working

Readers run out of battery, phones lose signal, and Wi-Fi drops out. It happens to everyone sooner or later. A simple backup plan means you do not lose the sale.

  1. Charge everything the night before: the reader, your phone, and a small portable charger.
  2. Keep a small cash float. This just means some coins and small notes for giving change, so you can still take cash.
  3. Have a payment link ready. If your reader fails but the customer's phone works, you can text them a link to pay on their own phone.
  4. Know your signal. At a market or in a rural area, check the phone signal before the day. Some readers can store a few payments and send them later when the signal comes back; check whether yours does.
  5. Stay calm and honest. If nothing works, a polite "I'm sorry, my card machine is down today, can you pay by cash or bank transfer?" is perfectly fine.

A simple way to start

If you are not sure where to begin, keep it simple. Choose one well-known provider with no monthly fee, order a small reader, and try it at your next market or with your next few customers. You can always add a till system or payment links later as your business grows.

Taking cards is not about becoming a tech expert. It is about making it easy for people to buy from you. Once it is set up, most owners wonder why they waited so long.

Common questions

How much does it cost to take card payments?

Usually there is a small cost for the card reader, and then a small percentage of each card sale, called a transaction fee. For many simple card reader services this is often somewhere around 1 to 3 percent of each sale, but it varies by provider, country and type of card. Always check the provider's current price list before you sign up.

Do I need a business bank account to take card payments?

Many payment providers let you start with a personal account in your own name, but it is wiser to use a separate business account. It keeps your business money apart and makes your tax records much easier.

Can customers pay with their phone?

Yes. Most modern card readers accept contactless payments, which includes phones and watches using Apple Pay or Google Pay. The customer simply holds their phone near the reader.

How long does it take for card payments to reach my bank?

It depends on the provider. Many pay money into your bank account within one to a few working days. Check the provider's payout times before you choose, especially if you need the money quickly.

What happens if a customer disputes a card payment?

This is called a chargeback: the customer asks their bank to take the money back. The payment provider will ask you for proof, such as a receipt or an email. Keeping clear records of every sale helps you deal with these.
