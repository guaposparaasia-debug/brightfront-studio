How to turn your career experience into a consulting business

After 20 or 30 years in one field, you know things that smaller organisations would happily pay to borrow. Consulting is one of the most common, lowest-cost ways to start a business after 40. Here is how to do it properly, from choosing a niche to landing your first clients.

In short

  • Specialise. "Operations consultant for family-run manufacturers" wins more work than "business consultant".
  • Sell a clear outcome in a fixed package, not just your hours.
  • Your former employers, clients and colleagues are your best first source of work.
  • Check your employment contract for non-compete or confidentiality terms before you approach anyone.

Why consulting suits experienced people

Consulting is simply selling your expertise to organisations that need it for a while, but not full-time. A small manufacturer may need someone to sort out its supply chain for three months. A growing charity may need an HR policy written. A family business may need help preparing for a sale. They cannot justify hiring a senior expert permanently, but they can pay you for a project.

It suits people over 40 for several reasons:

  • Low start-up cost. A laptop, a phone, a simple website and insurance are often enough.
  • Experience is the product. The longer you have done something, the more valuable your advice.
  • Flexible hours. You can take on one client or five, and slow down gradually towards retirement.
  • Your network is your marketing. Most consulting work comes through referrals and reputation.

Step 1: Check what you're allowed to do

Before you contact anyone, read your employment contract and any settlement or exit agreement. Look for:

  • Non-compete clauses that limit working for competitors for a period.
  • Non-solicitation clauses that stop you approaching your employer's clients or staff.
  • Confidentiality terms covering information you cannot use or share.
  • Intellectual property terms about materials you created at work.

How enforceable these are depends on where you live. If anything looks restrictive, a short consultation with an employment lawyer is cheap insurance.

Also check whether your field is regulated. Giving certain financial, legal, medical, engineering or immigration advice requires a licence in most countries.

Step 2: Choose a niche you can own

The biggest mistake new consultants make is being too general. "I'm a business consultant" makes people ask "what kind?" A specific niche makes it obvious who should hire you and why.

A strong niche combines a problem you solve with a type of client:

Examples of general versus specific consulting niches
Too generalSpecific and memorable
HR consultantHR policies and first hires for businesses growing past 10 staff
Marketing consultantMarketing plans for independent dental and physio practices
IT consultantCybersecurity basics for accountancy and law firms under 20 people
Operations consultantWarehouse and stock systems for family-run food producers
Education consultantInspection preparation for small private schools

You can still take other work. The niche is what you lead with, so people remember you and refer you.

Quick niche test

Could a friend introduce you in one sentence that makes the right client say "I need to talk to them"? If yes, your niche is clear enough.

Step 3: Package your offer

Clients find it easier to buy a defined package than an open-ended number of hours. Packages also protect you from projects that drag on forever. Three common formats:

  1. A diagnostic or audit. A short, fixed-price review: "In two weeks I'll review your hiring process and give you a written report with ten prioritised fixes." This is often the easiest first sale.
  2. A project. A defined piece of work with a clear outcome and timeline: writing a staff handbook, setting up a new stock system, preparing for an audit.
  3. A retainer. Ongoing support for a monthly fee: a set number of hours, a monthly meeting, or being "on call" for advice. Retainers give you predictable income.

Write a one-page description of each package: what is included, what the client gets at the end, how long it takes and what it costs.

Step 4: Set your fees

New consultants almost always undercharge. A common error is taking your old salary and dividing it by working hours. That ignores the fact that you now pay for your own holidays, sick days, pension, equipment, insurance and the many unpaid hours spent on admin and finding work.

As a rough guide, many independent consultants find they can only bill about half of their working hours. The rest goes on marketing, proposals, travel and admin. Your rate needs to cover that.

Better still, price on value. If your stock system saves a client $40,000 a year, a fee of $8,000 is an easy decision for them, however many hours it takes you. We explain both methods in how to price your services when you're starting out.

Step 5: Set up the essentials

  • Legal structure: sole proprietor or sole trader to start, or a limited company or LLC. Ask an accountant.
  • Professional indemnity insurance, which covers you if a client claims your advice cost them money. Some clients will require it.
  • A simple contract or terms of business: scope, fees, payment terms, confidentiality, and what happens if the project changes.
  • A separate business bank account and a way to send professional invoices.
  • A professional email address on your own domain.
  • A simple website and an updated LinkedIn profile that match each other.

What your consulting website needs

Clients who hear about you through a referral will look you up. Your website does not need to be big, but it should answer their questions in under a minute:

  • Who you help and the problem you solve, in the first line.
  • Your packages, with a starting price or "from" price if you are comfortable sharing it.
  • Your background: years of experience, organisations you worked for (if allowed), a professional photo.
  • Two or three short client testimonials or examples of results, once you have them.
  • A clear way to book a call or send an email.

Step 6: Win your first clients

Most consultants win their first clients through people they already know. A practical plan:

  1. Make a list of 50 people who know your work: former managers, colleagues who moved companies, clients, suppliers, people from industry groups.
  2. Send a short, personal message to each: what you now do, who you help, and a simple request. "If you know anyone dealing with X, I'd be grateful for an introduction."
  3. Offer a useful first conversation. A free 30-minute call to understand their situation, with no pressure.
  4. Follow up once after two weeks, politely. Most work comes from the follow-up, not the first message.
  5. Share what you know. Post a short, practical tip on LinkedIn each week, speak at a local business group, or write a helpful article. This keeps you in people's minds.

Step 7: Do great work and get it in writing

Your first few projects are your marketing. Over-deliver on communication: confirm what you agreed in writing, give regular short updates, and finish on time. At the end, ask:

  • "Would you be happy for me to quote you on my website?"
  • "Is there anyone else who might benefit from this kind of help?"
  • "Would it be useful to check in again in three months?"

That last question often turns a one-off project into a retainer.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Being a generalist. Specialise in what you lead with.
  • Working without a written agreement. Even a one-page email confirming scope and fees prevents misunderstandings.
  • Letting projects grow without changing the price. When the scope changes, agree a new fee.
  • Relying on one client. A single large client is risky. Aim for at least three.
  • Stopping marketing when busy. Keep a little time each week for staying in touch, so the next project is ready when this one ends.

Consulting rewards exactly what you have spent decades building: knowledge, judgement and relationships. Package it clearly, price it fairly and let your network know.

Common questions

Do I need qualifications to become a consultant?

In most fields, no formal qualification is needed to call yourself a consultant. Your track record is what clients buy. Some areas, such as financial advice, law, accounting, engineering and health, are regulated, and you must hold the right licence to give certain advice.

How much should a new consultant charge?

Research what others in your field and region charge, then price for the value you deliver rather than your old salary divided by hours. Many new consultants start too low. Our guide to pricing your services walks through the maths.

Can I consult for my former employer?

Often yes, and it can be an excellent first contract. Check your employment contract and any exit agreement for non-compete, non-solicitation or confidentiality clauses first, and get legal advice if you are unsure.

Do consultants need a website?

Yes, a simple one. Clients will look you up after a referral. A clean page explaining who you help, what you do, your background and how to contact you builds trust quickly. A LinkedIn profile alone is often not enough.
