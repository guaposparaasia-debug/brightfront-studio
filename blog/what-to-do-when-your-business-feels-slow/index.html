Pitfalls & confidence

What to do when your new business feels slow at first

You have opened your doors, told people, maybe built a website, and the phone is quiet. It is a worrying feeling, especially if you have put savings and hope into this. A slow start is very common, and it does not mean you made a mistake. This guide helps you work out what is going on and what to do next, one calm step at a time.

In short

  • Most new businesses start slowly. People need time to hear about you and to trust you.
  • Run three simple checks: can people find you, do they understand what you offer, and can they understand your price?
  • Try small, cheap changes one at a time, and give each one a few weeks.
  • Change your offer when the checks point to a real problem. Keep going when the signs are good but slow.

First, a slow start is normal

When you start a business, you know all about it. Nobody else does yet. People have to hear about you, then remember you, then need what you sell, and then decide to trust you. That takes time.

Think about how you choose a new hairdresser, builder or bookkeeper. You probably ask a friend, look online, read a few reviews, and wait until you actually need one. Your future customers are doing the same. Many of them may already have noticed you but are not ready to buy yet.

So a quiet first few weeks, or even months, does not mean your idea is bad. It is often just the time it takes for word to spread. Starting later in life does not change this. If you are worried about your age, our honest answer to is 50 too old to start a business? may help.

Be kind to yourself

Feeling anxious when it is quiet is natural. It helps to remember that you are in the early, slow part that almost every business goes through. It does not last forever if you keep going and keep learning.

Slow start or real problem? How to tell

A slow start and a real problem can feel the same from the inside. The difference shows up in the details. Look at what is happening over several weeks, not just the last few days.

Signs of a normal slow start compared with signs of a real problem
Signs of a normal slow startSigns something needs to change
A few people ask questions, even if they do not buy yetAlmost nobody gets in touch at all, even after you have told many people
Customers who do buy are happy and some come backCustomers are unhappy, or do not come back
Things are slowly getting a little better each monthNothing has changed for several months
People say "not right now" or "maybe later"People keep saying no for the same reason
Your costs are covered, or close to itYou keep spending money you cannot afford to lose

If most of what you see is in the left column, you probably need patience and steady effort. If several things are in the right column, it is time to look harder at your offer, your price or how people find you.

Three simple checks to run

Before you change anything big, run these three checks. They are free, they take an afternoon, and they often show the real reason things are quiet.

Check 1: Can people find you?

People cannot buy from you if they do not know you exist. Try this:

  • Search for your business name on Google. Does it come up?
  • Search for what you do and your town, for example "dog walker Millbrook". Do you appear at all?
  • Is your free Google Business Profile set up? This is the listing that puts you on Google Maps. Our step-by-step guide shows how.
  • Do you have a simple website, or at least one page, that people can find after hearing your name?
  • Have you told everyone you know, one by one, what you now do?

Many slow starts come down to this check alone. Most people who are just starting have told far fewer people than they think.

Check 2: Is your offer clear?

Your "offer" simply means what you sell and who it is for. Ask a friend to look at your website or leaflet for ten seconds, then put it away and tell you:

  • What do you do?
  • Who is it for?
  • Where do you work?
  • How do they get in touch?

If they cannot answer all four quickly, your message needs to be clearer. A good test is one simple sentence: "I help [who] with [what] so they can [result]." Our guide on getting your first customers explains how to write it.

Check 3: Is your price easy to understand?

People often go quiet when they cannot see a price, or cannot work out what they will end up paying. Check that:

  • Your prices, or at least a starting price, are easy to find.
  • It is clear what is included in each price.
  • There are no surprise extra costs at the end.
  • Your price makes sense next to what others nearby charge.

Notice that this check is about being clear, not about being cheap. If you are unsure whether your price is right, our guide on how to price your services walks through it, and our free pricing calculator can help with the sums.

Simple, low-cost things to try

Once the checks are done, pick one or two of these. Try them properly for a few weeks before judging them. Changing everything at once makes it hard to know what helped.

  1. Tell more people, personally. Send short, friendly messages to people you know. Ask them to pass your name on to anyone who might need you. A personal message works much better than a general post.
  2. Ask happy customers for a review. Even two or three honest reviews make strangers more willing to try you. Our guide to getting your first customer reviews has wording you can use.
  3. Make it easier to say yes. Offer a small first job, a short first session or a simple starter package, so trying you feels less risky.
  4. Show your work. Add a few real photos of what you do to your website and Google profile. People like to see before they buy.
  5. Team up with a related business. A gardener and a garden centre, a bookkeeper and an accountant. Agree to recommend each other.
  6. Show up locally. Join a local business group, put a card on a community noticeboard, or give a short free talk at the library on something you know well.
  7. Follow up. If someone asked about your service and went quiet, send one polite message. People get busy and forget.

None of these cost much. Most cost only your time. Write down what you tried and when, so you can see later what made a difference.

When to change your offer and when to wait

This is the hardest decision, and there is no perfect rule. These signs can help you decide.

It is probably time to change something if:

  • Several people say no for the same reason, for example "too expensive", "you are too far away" or "I don't need all of that".
  • People are interested in a different service from the one you planned. That is useful news, not failure.
  • You have run the three checks, tried several low-cost things for a few months, and very little has changed.

It is probably better to keep going if:

  • Things are slowly getting better, even if slower than you hoped.
  • The customers you do have are happy and some recommend you.
  • You have only been trying for a few weeks.

Changing your offer does not have to mean starting again. Often it is a small adjustment: a different price, a smaller first package, a narrower group of customers, or a slightly different service. Make one change at a time and give it a fair chance.

Look at your plan again

If you wrote a one-page business plan, now is a good time to read it. Check the spending limit you set yourself, and update the page with what you have learned from real customers.

Protect your money and your energy

A slow start is easier to get through if you are not under money pressure. A few things help:

  • Stick to the spending limit you set before you started. If you did not set one, set one now.
  • Avoid big new costs while things are quiet, such as long contracts or expensive advertising you have not tested.
  • Keep other income going if you can, such as part-time work. Our guide to starting a side business without quitting your job covers this.
  • Talk to someone. Another business owner, a mentor or a friend can often see things you cannot. In the US, SCORE offers free mentoring, and many countries have similar free services.

Look after yourself too. Keep regular hours, take a day off each week, and notice the small wins.

A short weekly routine to keep going

When things are quiet, a simple routine stops you drifting and keeps you moving forward. It takes about an hour a week.

A simple weekly routine for a quiet period in a new business
DayWhat to doTime
MondaySend three personal messages to people who might need you or know someone who does15 minutes
WednesdayFollow up anyone who asked about your service but has not replied10 minutes
ThursdayDo one thing to be easier to find: a new photo, a Google profile update, a card on a noticeboard20 minutes
FridayWrite down how many people got in touch, what they asked and any reasons for saying no15 minutes

At the end of each month, read your Friday notes. Look for patterns. Are more people getting in touch than last month? Do the same questions keep coming up? Your notes will tell you what to try next far better than guessing.

Keep going, calmly

Almost every business has a quiet start. The owners who get through it usually do the same few things: they make sure people can find them, they keep their offer and price clear, they try small changes one at a time, and they keep showing up each week.

You have more experience, patience and good sense than you may give yourself credit for. Use them. If you want a reminder of the bigger picture, our step-by-step guide to starting a business after 40 is a good place to look again.

Common questions

How long is it normal for a new business to be slow?

There is no fixed answer. It depends on your type of business, where you are, and how many people already know you. Many new owners find the first few months quiet while word spreads. What matters more is whether things are slowly getting better.

Should I lower my prices if business is slow?

Not as a first step. Check first that people can find you and understand what you offer. Cutting prices can bring in the wrong customers and leave you working hard for little money. See our guide on how to price your services.

How do I know if my business idea is not working?

Warning signs include people regularly saying no for the same reason, almost no one asking about you even after you have told many people, or costs you cannot keep covering. One quiet week is not a sign. A steady pattern over a few months is.

Is it too late to change my business idea?

No. Many owners adjust what they sell, who they sell to or how they price it in the first year. Small changes based on what real customers tell you are a normal part of starting out.
