What diners look for before they visit

Most people who look up a restaurant are not browsing. They are standing on a corner, sitting in a car or planning tonight, and they have three or four questions. Are you open right now? What do you serve, and roughly what does it cost? Where exactly are you, and is there parking? Can I book, order ahead or get delivery? A good restaurant website answers those questions in the first scroll, on a phone, without making anyone pinch, zoom or download a file.

Everything else, like the story of the family recipe, the chef's background or the sourcing of your coffee, is valuable once the basics are clear. It is what turns a quick check into a decision to come in. But it works best underneath the essentials, not in front of them.

The sections a restaurant site usually needs

Most restaurants, cafes and bakeries are well served by one well-organized page. Here is the order we usually suggest, and why:

Welcome and today's hours

Your name, one line about what you are, a strong photo, and today's hours where nobody can miss them.

Menu

Typed out as real text, grouped the way you serve it: breakfast, lunch, drinks, bakery case, specials.

Photos

Your best dishes, the room, the counter or the front door so people recognize you when they arrive.

Order, book or call

Buttons to call, get directions, and open the booking or ordering service you already use.

About

A few honest sentences about who runs the place and what you care about.

Location and details

Address, a map link, parking or transit notes, accessibility, dog-friendly or not, cash or card.

Menus that work on a phone

The single most common problem on small restaurant websites is the menu. A photo of the paper menu or a linked PDF is hard to read on a small screen, slow to load on mobile data, and invisible to search engines, which cannot read the text inside an image. A menu typed as real text fixes all three.

  • Group it the way you serve it. Breakfast, mains, sides, drinks, pastries. Short headings make a long menu easy to scan.
  • Prices are your call. Showing prices builds trust and saves phone calls. If your prices change often, you can leave them off or show only the items that rarely change.
  • Mark dietary options clearly. Vegetarian, vegan or gluten-free options are some of the most searched details. Only label what you can stand behind.
  • Keep specials separate. A short "this season" or "this week" block is easier to update than reprinting the whole menu.

When the menu changes, you email us the new version and we update it. That is part of the $49/month Care Plan, not an extra job.

Hours, holidays and the "are you open?" question

Wrong hours cost real customers. Someone who drives over and finds the door locked rarely tries again. Put your regular hours in one clear table, and plan for exceptions: holidays, private events, early closing for staff training, seasonal hours. Because changes are covered by maintenance, keeping the site accurate is a matter of sending a one-line email when something changes.

It also helps to keep the same hours everywhere: your website, your Google Business Profile and your social accounts. When the three disagree, customers do not know which one to trust.

Photos that make people hungry

Phone photos are enough to start. A few simple habits make them much better:

  • Shoot near a window in daylight, and turn off the overhead lights if they make food look yellow.
  • Take one photo of each of your most popular dishes, from slightly above.
  • Get one wide shot of the room when it looks its best, and one of the outside so people recognize the building.
  • Skip heavy filters. People want to see what they will actually get.

We crop and size the photos for the page so it stays fast, and you can send replacements any time.

Cafes, bakeries and restaurants are a little different

Cafes often live on regulars and the morning rush, so opening time, seating, Wi-Fi and whether you are laptop-friendly matter. Bakeries are usually about what is available and when: what sells out early, which days have which breads, and how to order cakes in advance. Restaurants tend to need reservations, larger menus and information for groups and special occasions. Tell us which of these sounds like you, and we will arrange the page around what your customers ask most.

Common questions from restaurant owners

Can I keep my PDF menu?

You can, but we recommend typing the menu into the page as real text. It is easier to read on a phone, loads faster and can be found by search engines. If you want, we can also link the PDF for people who prefer to print it.

Can customers order or book from the website?

We link your website to the ordering, delivery or booking service you already use, so customers can tap straight through. Those services have their own fees. Taking payments directly on your website is not part of our offer.

How do I change the specials every week?

Email us the new specials. Updates like that are covered by the $49/month Care Plan. A short, separate specials block makes this quick.

We have two locations. Is that one website?

Tell us in your request. Depending on how different the two places are, one page with both addresses and hours may be enough, or each location may deserve its own section.

Can you add our Google reviews?

We can link to your Google or other review profiles. We do not write or invent reviews, and we do not copy reviews onto the site without permission.

Do I have to show prices?

No. Many places show them because it saves questions, but it is your decision. You can also show prices only for items that rarely change.
