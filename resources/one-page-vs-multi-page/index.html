One page or several pages for a local shop?

Most small shops are well served by one clear page with everything a customer needs. Separate pages start to help when you have a lot to say about clearly different things. Here is how to tell which you need.

In short

  • One page suits most cafes, salons, food trucks and small shops: it is fast and easy to keep accurate.
  • Separate pages help with long menus, several locations, distinct services or regular news.
  • Start simple. You can add pages later as the business grows.

Why one page often works best

  • It is quick on a phone. Visitors scroll once and see your photos, hours, menu or services, and how to reach you.
  • Nothing gets lost. There are no menus to dig through and no forgotten page with old information.
  • It is easy to keep accurate. One place to update when your hours or prices change.
  • It loads fast. One request, no clicking around on slow mobile data.

The anatomy of a good one-page site

A one-page website is not one long block of text. It is a sequence of short sections, each answering one question, with a menu at the top that jumps to each section. A typical order for a local shop:

  1. Welcome: your name, one line about what you do, a strong photo, and the main button (call, book or order).
  2. What you offer: menu, services or product categories, with prices if you show them.
  3. Photos: your best work, products or dishes, and your space.
  4. About: a few sentences about who runs the place.
  5. Hours and location: a clear hours table, address and a directions link.
  6. Contact: phone, email or message buttons, and links to your social accounts.

For a restaurant the menu moves up; for a salon, prices and booking; for a food truck, where to find you this week. See our guides for restaurants, salons and food trucks.

When separate pages make sense

  • You have a long menu or many services that deserve their own page.
  • You serve clearly different groups, for example walk-in customers and catering or events.
  • You have several locations, each with its own hours and address.
  • You want to publish news, events or seasonal offers regularly.
  • You need longer policy or information pages, like a detailed booking policy or allergen information.

What about search engines?

A common worry is that one page will hurt how you appear on Google. For a local shop, the things that matter most are usually having your real name, location, hours and what you offer written clearly as text, and keeping your Google Business Profile complete and consistent with your website. A focused single page can do that well. Separate pages can help when you genuinely offer distinct things that people search for separately, for example a bakery that also does wedding cakes. Nobody can honestly promise rankings either way; clarity and accuracy are what you control.

Two quick examples

A neighborhood cafe with a short breakfast and lunch menu, one location and regular hours: one page is ideal. Welcome, menu, photos, about, hours and location, contact.

A bakery that also makes custom celebration cakes: the daily bakery fits on the main page, but cake orders involve sizes, flavors, lead times and an order inquiry. A second page for custom cakes keeps the main page simple and gives cake customers everything they need.

A food truck with a weekly schedule and a catering side: one page for the menu, where to find you and photos, with a clear catering section near the end. If catering grows into a big part of the business, it can move to its own page later.

A simple way to decide

Write down what a first-time customer needs to know before they visit. If it fits comfortably on one scrolling page, start with one page. If one topic needs much more space than the others, give that topic its own page. You can add more later as the business grows; with maintenance included, that is a conversation, not a rebuild. Quick checklist before you decide

  • Can a first-time customer find your hours, location and contact in one scroll?
  • Is any single topic, like a long menu or catering, much bigger than the rest?
  • Do you have more than one location with different hours?
  • Will you publish news or events regularly?
  • Can you, or someone you pay, keep every page accurate?

If you answered "no" to the middle three, one page is almost certainly the right start.

Common questions

Is a one-page website less professional?

No. A clear, well-designed single page often feels more professional than several thin pages, because everything a customer needs is in one place.

Can I add pages later?

Yes. Many shops start with one page and add a page when something grows, like catering or custom orders.

Does a one-page site work for a salon with many services?

Usually yes, with services grouped under short headings. If your list is very long, a separate services page can help.
