The three common ways to get a website

1. Build it yourself on a website builder

You pay the builder a monthly or yearly subscription, pick a template and do the writing, photos and layout yourself. The subscription keeps the site online. Every later change, like new hours, a new menu or a new price, is also yours to make. Builders are good products; the trade-off is that the labor is yours.

2. Pay a freelancer once, then per change

Many low-cost freelancers advertise a flat build price with "no monthly fee". That is real, but hosting usually still has to be paid by someone, and updates are often billed one at a time. A shop that changes its menu or hours a few times a year pays for each of those, and waits for each one.

3. A build fee plus a small monthly that includes changes

That is how we work: $250 to start, $250 after you approve the finished site, then $39/month that covers hosting, updates and changes you ask for. No per-change fees, and it is month to month.

Year by year, the picture changes

In year one, the builder usually looks cheapest because there is no build fee. By year two the costs converge, and from then on the difference depends almost entirely on how often your information changes and who makes the changes. A business whose website never changes might be fine with a per-change freelancer. A cafe that updates its menu each season, adjusts holiday hours and swaps photos a few times a year will find per-change bills add up, and a builder will quietly consume evenings.

The cost people forget: the owner's time

A builder can look cheapest because your time is not on the bill. Setting up a decent site usually takes several evenings: choosing a template, writing every section, resizing photos, fixing the phone layout, connecting the domain. Then someone has to keep it right. If you value an hour of your time at even a modest rate, a few hours a month adds up quickly. It also tends not to happen in a busy week, which is how websites end up showing last year's hours.

If you enjoy building things and have the time, a builder is a perfectly good choice. If you would rather spend that time running the shop, paying someone who includes changes is often the better deal.

Costs that are easy to miss

  • Add-ons inside builders. Some features you may want, like removing the builder's branding, extra storage or certain integrations, can sit on higher plans. Check which plan you actually need, not just the cheapest one advertised.
  • Renewal prices. Introductory prices for plans and domains sometimes rise at renewal. Look at the renewal price, not only the first-year price.
  • Separate hosting. A "no monthly fee" build still has to live somewhere. If hosting is in your name, you are responsible for paying and renewing it.
  • The cost of being wrong. Outdated hours or an old menu do not show up on any invoice, but they cost customers who arrive to a closed door.

When each option makes sense

  • Builder: you like hands-on work, have regular free time and want full control over every detail.
  • Per-change freelancer: your information almost never changes, and you are happy to wait for each update.
  • Build fee plus maintenance: your hours, menu, prices or photos change several times a year, and you want one predictable monthly cost with someone else doing the work.

Questions to ask any provider

  • What is the total for the first year, and for each year after?
  • Are changes included, or billed one by one? What counts as a change?
  • Who pays for hosting, and is it included?
  • Is there a contract, or can I cancel month to month?
  • What happens to the site and my domain name if I stop paying?
