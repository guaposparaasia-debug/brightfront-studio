Why there is no single answer

You may have seen headlines claiming that a business costs "just $100" or "at least $50,000" to start. Both can be true, for different businesses. A bookkeeper working from a spare room and a family opening a cafe are both "starting a small business", but their costs are worlds apart.

So instead of looking for one number, it is more useful to learn what kinds of costs exist and then price them for your own idea. The ranges in this article are typical examples to help you think, not quotes or guarantees. Real costs depend heavily on your location, your trade and the choices you make.

One-off costs and monthly costs

Every business has two kinds of costs, and it helps to keep them on separate lists.

  • One-off (start-up) costs are what you spend before your first sale: equipment, initial stock, registration, a website, signage, training, deposits.
  • Monthly (running) costs are what it costs to keep the business open, even in a quiet month: insurance, phone, software, rent, website hosting, fuel, loan repayments.

The one-off list tells you how much you need to start. The monthly list tells you how much you need to keep going until customers cover it. Many new owners plan the first carefully and underestimate the second.

Typical costs for a small service business

Service businesses, where you sell your skills and time, are usually the least expensive to start. Think of consulting, tutoring, bookkeeping, cleaning, garden care, pet sitting or coaching.

Typical costs for a shop, cafe or food business

Businesses with premises, stock or food preparation are a different scale. They often need many of the same basics as a service business, plus:

  • Premises: rent, a deposit (often several months), and sometimes key money or legal fees for the lease.
  • Fit-out: counters, shelving, lighting, flooring, signage, decoration.
  • Equipment: for food, this can include commercial ovens, fridges, coffee machines, extraction and sinks, which are often expensive.
  • Opening stock: everything you need on the shelves on day one.
  • Permits and inspections: food hygiene, health, fire safety, signage and sometimes planning permission.
  • Staff: wages and training before and after opening.
  • Utilities: often much higher than a home-based business.

For these businesses, start-up costs can easily run into tens of thousands of dollars, and more for a full restaurant or a large fit-out. That is not a reason to avoid them, but it is a reason to test the idea first, perhaps with a market stall, pop-up, food truck or catering orders, before signing a long lease.

Get real quotes early

For any business with premises or equipment, get at least two written quotes for the big items before you commit. Estimates from the internet are rarely accurate for your street and your trade.

The costs people forget

The obvious costs are rarely the problem. These are the ones that surprise new owners:

  • Insurance. Public liability, professional indemnity, equipment, vehicle use for business, or contents cover. Your home or car insurance may not cover business use.
  • Payment fees. Card readers and online payment services usually take a small percentage of every sale.
  • Accounting help. Even if you keep your own books, an accountant for the year-end and tax return is money well spent.
  • Tax set-aside. Some of what you earn will be owed in tax. It is not yours to spend, so put a share aside from every payment.
  • Subscriptions that creep up. Software, apps and services that each cost a little but add up across a year.
  • Deposits. Rent, equipment hire and some suppliers ask for money upfront.
  • Marketing basics. Signage, printing, a few ads or a listing fee.
  • The slow start. Customers rarely arrive all at once. You may pay several months of running costs before income is steady.
  • Your own time. If you are leaving a job, the business may not pay you properly for a while. Your household bills still need paying.

How to build your own start-up cost list

You can make a realistic estimate in an afternoon. A sheet of paper or a simple spreadsheet is fine.

  1. Describe the smallest version of your business that could serve paying customers. Not the dream version, the first version.
  2. List every one-off cost you need for that version. Walk through a typical customer from first contact to payment and note everything you need along the way.
  3. List every monthly cost, including the ones above that people forget.
  4. Price each line with real quotes or real prices where you can. Mark anything that is still a guess.
  5. Multiply the monthly total by the number of months you expect before the business covers its own costs. If you don't know, use a cautious guess such as six months.
  6. Add a buffer for surprises (see below).
  7. Add it up: one-off costs + months of running costs + buffer = the money you need available.

Keep a buffer for surprises

Something will cost more than you expected, or take longer. A tool breaks, a permit takes an extra month, a supplier raises prices, or the first customers come more slowly than hoped. A buffer means these things are annoying rather than frightening.

There is no perfect size. Some people add 10 to 20 percent to their total; others prefer a fixed amount, such as two or three extra months of running costs. Choose what lets you sleep at night, and keep the buffer separate so it doesn't quietly become part of the spending budget.

Alongside the buffer, set a spending limit: the most you are willing to put into the business before it pays for itself. Decide it now, while you are calm. If you reach it, stop and review before spending more.

Ways to start lean

You do not have to spend the whole amount on day one. Many of the most sensible businesses start small and grow from their own income.

  • Start with a service before adding products, stock or premises.
  • Use what you already have: your computer, phone, car, tools and kitchen, where rules allow.
  • Test before you invest. Sell to a few people, run a market stall, or do one paid job before buying equipment for a hundred.
  • Rent, borrow or buy second-hand for expensive equipment until you know you'll use it.
  • Avoid long commitments, such as leases and loans, until demand is proven.
  • Keep your job, or part of it, while the business grows. Our guide to starting a side business without quitting your job explains how.
  • Keep the online setup simple: a clear one-page website and a free Google Business Profile are enough for many local businesses.
  • Price properly from the start, so each sale covers its share of your costs. See how to price your services.

Starting lean is not about being cheap. It is about spending money only once you know it will help.

Protect your savings

If you are starting after 40, there is less time to rebuild savings than at 25. A few principles help:

  • Don't drain retirement accounts to fund a business. Early withdrawals can carry tax penalties, and that money is meant to look after you later.
  • Be very careful with loans secured on your home.
  • Keep business money in a separate account, so you always know what the business has really cost.
  • Get advice from an accountant or financial adviser before using large savings, a redundancy payment or a pension lump sum.

Putting it all together

The real cost of starting your business is not a number you find online. It is the total of your own one-off costs, a few months of running costs, and a sensible buffer, worked out for the smallest version of your idea. For a service business that may be modest; for a shop or food business it will be much more, which is a good reason to test small first.
