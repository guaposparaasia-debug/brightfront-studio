Why your name matters, and why it does not need to be clever

A business name has a simple job. It helps people remember you, find you, and tell their friends about you. That is all.

It does not need to be funny, unusual or "creative". In fact, many of the best small business names are plain. They say who you are, what you do, or where you are. "Millbrook Bakery" or "Sarah Hill Garden Care" may not win prizes, but people know exactly what they are and can find them easily.

What really matters is that your name is easy to say, easy to find, and not already being used by someone else. Get those three things right and you will not regret your choice.

Step 1: Make a long list of name ideas

Do not try to find the perfect name in your head. Sit down with a notebook and write down every idea, even the silly ones. Aim for at least twenty. Your best name is rarely the first one you think of.

Here are some easy starting points:

  • Your own name: "Hill Bookkeeping", "Patel Home Repairs".
  • Your town or area: "Riverside Dog Walking", "Northgate Tutors".
  • What you do, said simply: "The Sewing Room", "Fresh Start Cleaning".
  • What customers get: "Tidy Gardens", "Stress-Free Moves".
  • Something personal: a family name, a street, a nickname, or a word that means something to you, as long as customers can say it.

You can also mix these: a place plus what you do, or your name plus what you do. If you get stuck, an AI helper can suggest more ideas to add to your list. Our beginner's guide to AI tools shows how. Just treat its ideas as a starting point, and check them with the steps below.

Step 2: Keep it easy to say and spell

Most new customers will hear your name before they see it. A friend will say "Try the woman who does garden care in Millbrook". If the person cannot spell your name when they go to search for it, you lose them.

Cross off any ideas that fail these simple tests:

  • The phone test: say the name out loud as if answering the phone. Is it easy to say?
  • The spelling test: say it to someone and ask them to write it down. Did they spell it right?
  • Not too long: could it fit on the side of a van, a small sign or a business card?
  • No odd spellings: "Kwik Klean" or "Phresh" look fun but are hard to find. People will type the normal spelling.
  • No confusing numbers or symbols: is it "4" or "four"? "&" or "and"? Avoid making people guess.
  • No words that sound like something else you would not want to be linked with.

Keep the names that pass. You will probably be down to a shortlist of five to ten.

Step 3: Check no one else is using it

This is the step people most often skip, and the one most likely to cause regret. If another business is already using your name, or something very close, you may confuse customers, lose them to the other business, or even be asked to stop using it.

For each name on your shortlist:

  1. Search for it on Google, both on its own and with your town and what you do. Look at Google Maps too.
  2. Check your government's business register. This is the official list of registered business or company names. Most countries and many states have a free online search.
  3. Check for trademarks. A trademark is a legal right to a name for a certain kind of business. In the US you can search the official trademark records at the US Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) website; the UK and many other countries have their own free search. You are looking for the same or a very similar name used for the same kind of work.
  4. Look at social media, such as Facebook and Instagram, for businesses with the same or a similar name.

A name used by a dentist in another country is usually not a problem for a dog walker in your town. A name used by another dog walker two towns away is. If you are unsure, an hour with a lawyer or a business adviser is cheaper than changing your name later.

Watch out for scary letters

Once you register a name, you may get official-looking letters asking for money to "protect" or "register" it again. Many are not from the government. Our guide to scams that target new small business owners explains how to spot them.

Step 4: Check the web address and social names are free

Even if you do not plan a website yet, you will want one later. Your web address, also called a domain name, is the address people type to find you online, such as millbrookbakery.com. Check now that a good one is free for your name.

  • Search for the web address on any domain seller's website. It will tell you straight away if it is free.
  • Try the plain version first, such as yourbusinessname.com, or your country's ending.
  • If it is taken, try adding your town or what you do, such as millbrookbakeryco.com or bakerymillbrook.com. Avoid hyphens if you can; they are easy to forget when said out loud.
  • Check social media names. Your social name, sometimes called a "handle", is the name people search for on apps like Facebook and Instagram. See if your name, or something close, is free on the ones your customers use.

When you find a name with a free web address, buy the web address straight away. It usually costs a small amount each year, and it stops someone else taking it while you decide. Register it in your own name, keep the login safe, and turn on automatic renewal. Our guide to building a simple website explains more about web addresses.

Step 5: Test it on real people

You will hear your business name thousands of times. It is worth hearing what other people think before you decide. Pick your top two or three names and try them on people you trust, especially people like your future customers.

Ask simple questions:

  • "What do you think a business with this name does?"
  • "Can you spell it back to me?"
  • "Which of these would you remember tomorrow?"
  • "Does anything about it put you off?"

Listen more than you explain. If you find yourself saying "Well, the name means…", that is a sign the name may not be clear on its own. You will not be there to explain it to every customer.

Do not worry if a few people are not keen. Very few names get everyone excited. You are looking for names that are clear, easy to remember and do not cause confusion.

Step 6: Avoid names that box you in

Think about where your business might be in five years. A name that fits perfectly today can feel too small later.

Examples of business names that may box you in, and wider alternatives
May box you inWhyWider alternative
Millbrook Lawn MowingHard to add hedges, planting or garden design laterMillbrook Garden Care
Wedding Cakes by AnnHard to sell birthday cakes or breadAnn's Kitchen Bakery
Northgate Laptop RepairHard to add phones, printers or home Wi-Fi helpNorthgate Tech Help
Riverside Dog WalkingFine if you only ever plan to walk dogsRiverside Pet Care, if you may add pet sitting

These names are only examples to show the idea. The same goes for places: a name with your street or village in it is lovely, but may feel odd if you later serve the whole county.

That said, do not make your name so wide that it says nothing. "Smith Services" could be almost anything. A good balance is a name that says your general area of work, with room to grow inside it.

Step 7: Before you pay for signs and cards

Once you have a favourite, it is tempting to order signs, cards and a logo the same day. Slow down for a moment. Printing is where a rushed name gets expensive.

  • Sleep on it. Live with the name for a few days. Say it out loud. Write it on a piece of paper and stick it on the fridge. Does it still feel right?
  • Do the checks one last time: search, business register, trademarks, web address and social names.
  • Buy the web address and, if you can, set up the social names, so no one else takes them.
  • Register the name if you need to. In many places, if you trade under a name that is not your own legal name, you must register it. This is sometimes called a trading name or a "doing business as" name. Check your local government's small business website or ask an accountant.
  • Start small with printing. Order a small batch of cards first. You can order more once you are sure.
  • Keep your name the same everywhere: on your sign, cards, website, invoices and Google listing. Our Google Business Profile guide explains why this matters for being found.

Your name is also a good thing to write at the top of your one-page business plan. Seeing it there, next to what you sell and who you help, is a good final check that it fits.

A quick checklist to finish

Before you decide, make sure your chosen name passes every line:

  • Easy to say on the phone
  • Easy to spell after hearing it once
  • Not used by a similar business near you or online
  • No trademark problems that you can find
  • A good web address is free, and you have bought it
  • Social names are free, or close enough
  • Tested on a few real people
  • Leaves room for your business to grow
  • You still like it after a few days

If it passes, you have a name you can be proud of for years. Now it is time for the fun part: telling people about it. Our step-by-step guide to starting a business after 40 shows what comes next.
