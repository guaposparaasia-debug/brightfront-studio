Why reviews matter so much for a new business

When people consider a business they have never used, they look for signs that other people have used it and been happy. Established businesses have years of word of mouth behind them. A new business has nothing yet, which is exactly why the first few reviews carry so much weight.

Reviews help you in three ways:

  • Trust. A few honest comments from real customers reassure a stranger that you exist, turn up and do good work.
  • Being found. Reviews are part of what customers see on Google Search and Maps. Google says the number and quality of reviews are among the things that can affect how a local business appears. Our Google Business Profile guide explains the profile they appear on.
  • Feedback. Reviews tell you, in customers' own words, what they value. That helps you describe your business better on your website and in conversation.

Reviews will not replace good work, clear prices or a simple website. But for a new business, they are often the thing that tips a hesitant customer into getting in touch.

Where to collect reviews

Pick one main place to start, so your reviews build up in one spot rather than scattering. For most local businesses, that is your Google Business Profile, because it is what people see when they search for you or look at a map.

Depending on your trade, there may also be a review site customers in your field already use. That is fine, but start with one and add others only when the first is going well.

You can also quote short reviews on your website, with the customer's permission. Our guide to building a simple website covers where they fit.

The rules you must never break

Before the "how", the hard lines. These protect your customers, your reputation and your listing.

  • Never buy reviews. Not from agencies, not from websites, not from anyone.
  • Never write fake reviews, and never ask friends or family to review a service they have not actually used.
  • Never offer anything in exchange for a review: no discounts, gifts, free extras or prize draws. A review should be freely given.
  • Never review your competitors, positively or negatively.
  • Don't pick and choose who you ask. Ask every customer the same way, rather than only those you expect to be delighted.

Breaking these rules goes against the policies of major platforms, including Google. Reviews can be removed and listings restricted. In some countries, fake and paid-for reviews also break consumer protection law; in the US, for example, the Federal Trade Commission has rules against them. And customers are increasingly good at spotting reviews that don't ring true.

Thanking is fine; trading is not

You can always thank a customer for leaving a review. What you must not do is promise or give something in return for leaving one.

When to ask

The best moment to ask is soon after a good experience, while it is fresh and the customer is pleased. In practice that usually means:

  • At the end of a job, when the customer says thank you or tells you they are happy.
  • When you hand over an order or a finished piece of work.
  • The same day or the day after, in a short follow-up message.

Weeks later, people have moved on and the request feels like a chore. If you forget to ask at the time, a friendly message within a few days still works well.

Three easy ways to ask

1. In person

This feels the most awkward in your head and the least awkward in real life. When a customer is clearly pleased, a short, relaxed sentence is enough. You are not begging; you are inviting them to share their experience.

"I'm really glad you're happy with it. I've just started out, so reviews make a big difference to a small business like mine. Would you mind leaving a quick review on Google if you have a minute? I can text you the link."

Then send the link straight away, while you are still together, so it doesn't get forgotten.

2. A follow-up message

A short text or email after the job lets customers review in their own time. Keep it personal and brief, and put the link where they can tap it.

"Hi Margaret, thank you again for choosing us for your garden this week. If you have a minute, an honest review on Google would really help other people find a small local business like ours: [link]. Thank you!"

If they don't respond, you can send one gentle reminder a few days later. After that, leave it. Repeated nudging feels pushy.

"Hi Margaret, just a gentle reminder in case my last message got buried. If you'd like to leave a quick review, here's the link: [link]. No worries at all if not. Thanks again!"

3. A simple link or QR code

Your Google Business Profile gives you a short link that takes customers straight to the review box. Copy it once and keep it somewhere handy on your phone. You can also turn it into a QR code, using a free QR code tool or your profile's own sharing options where available, and print it:

  • On a small card you hand over with an order or leave after a visit.
  • On your receipts or invoices.
  • On a sign near your counter or till, if you have premises.

Keep the wording on cards and signs simple, with no reward attached:

"Enjoyed your visit? We'd love to hear about it. Scan to leave a review. Thank you for supporting a small local business."

Making it feel less awkward

Many people starting a business in their 40s, 50s or later feel uncomfortable asking for anything that looks like praise. A few things help:

  • Remember what you are asking for. You are asking for an honest opinion, not a compliment. If someone is unhappy, you would want to know.
  • Make it routine. Ask every customer in the same friendly way, as part of finishing the job. Routine takes the personal awkwardness out of it.
  • Use the "small business" angle honestly. Most people understand that reviews help a new business and are happy to help one they liked.
  • Make it effortless. A direct link turns a vague "I'll do it later" into a one-minute task.
  • Accept a "no" gracefully. Some people never write reviews. That's fine; thank them anyway.

What to do with your first reviews

  • Reply to every review, briefly and personally. A simple thank-you that mentions what they bought or the job you did shows you are real and you care.
  • Read them for useful words. If several customers praise how tidy you are or how quickly you reply, say so on your website and profile.
  • Quote them, with permission. A short line from a real review, with the customer's first name if they agree, can go on your website.
  • Pass on praise to anyone who helps you. If you have a helper or family member involved, share the kind words.
  • Keep asking. Recent reviews matter as well as the total, so make asking a permanent habit, not a one-off push.

A short, warm reply might look like this:

"Thank you so much, Margaret. It was a pleasure working on your garden, and I'm glad the new borders are looking good. See you in the spring!"

How to respond to a negative review calmly

Sooner or later, almost every business gets a disappointing review. It can feel very personal, especially when you have put your savings and heart into the business. How you respond matters more than the review itself, because future customers will read both.

  1. Wait before replying. Give yourself a few hours, or a night, so you reply calmly rather than defensively.
  2. Check the facts. Is it a real customer? What happened? Was there a misunderstanding or a genuine mistake?
  3. Reply briefly and politely. Thank them for the feedback, acknowledge their experience, and avoid arguing about details in public.
  4. Offer to put it right privately. Give a phone number or email so the conversation can continue away from the review page.
  5. Fix the cause if there is one, so it doesn't happen again.

"Thank you for taking the time to tell us about this, and I'm sorry your order wasn't what you expected. That isn't the standard we aim for. I'd like to put it right. Could you call me on [number] or email [address] so we can sort it out?"

Never reveal a customer's private details in a reply, never post angry responses, and never ask friends to leave reviews to "balance it out". If a review is fake, abusive, or clearly about a different business, report it through the platform's normal process rather than arguing with it.

A calm, helpful reply to a bad review often reassures future customers more than a page of perfect ones.
