Why a market is a good place to start

Selling at a market is one of the oldest and simplest ways to run a small business. You do not need a shop or a website on day one. You need a table, something good to sell, and a few hours on a Saturday.

A market also gives you something hard to get any other way: real customers, in person, telling you what they think. You will see which things they pick up, hear what they ask, and learn what they will pay. That is worth a lot when you are just starting out.

It suits many of the side business ideas for people over 40: home baking, preserves, plants, crafts, sewing, woodwork, vintage finds and more. It is also a low-risk way to test an idea before you spend money on a bigger setup.

Step 1: Find the right market and book a stall

Not every market suits every product. A farmers market wants fresh, local food. A craft fair wants handmade things. A general Sunday market sells a bit of everything.

  1. Visit as a shopper first. Go to one or two markets before you book. Walk around slowly. Which stalls are busy? What do people carry away? Is there anything like your product already?
  2. Talk to stallholders. Most are happy to chat when it is quiet. Ask how long they have been coming, and whether it is usually busy.
  3. Find the organiser. Look for a website, a social media page or a notice at the market. The local council or a community group often runs smaller markets.
  4. Ask the key questions: How much is a stall? What size is the space? Is a table or gazebo (a small pop-up tent) included? Is there electricity? What time is set-up and pack-down? Do I need insurance or any permits?
  5. Book a small space for one date. Do not sign up for a whole season until you have tried it once.
Check the rules for food

If you sell food or drink, you will usually need to follow food safety rules. You may also need to register with your local council or health department. Many markets also ask for public liability insurance, which covers you if a shopper is hurt because of your stall. Ask the organiser early, as these can take time to sort out.

Step 2: Decide what to bring

New stallholders often bring far too much stock, then spend the day worrying about it. A simpler approach works better.

  • Pick a small range. Three to six types of product is plenty for a first day. Too much choice can make people walk on.
  • Have a mix of prices. A few low-priced items that people can buy without thinking, a main range, and one or two special pieces.
  • Bring enough, not everything. Make a sensible guess, then add a little. It is fine to sell out early; it shows people your things are popular.

Keep a simple note of what you bring and what comes home. After a few markets, you will know much better how much to make.

Step 3: Set up your table so people stop

Shoppers decide in a few seconds whether to stop. They look from a few steps away, so your table needs to work from there, not just up close.

  • Use a plain tablecloth that reaches the ground. It looks tidy and hides your boxes underneath.
  • Add some height. Use crates, boxes, small shelves or baskets so things are not all flat on the table. Put taller items at the back.
  • Make a simple sign with your business name and what you sell, big enough to read from a few steps away. If you are still choosing a name, see how to choose a business name you will not regret.
  • Put a price on everything. Many people will not ask. If they cannot see a price, they often just walk on.
  • Keep it full but not crowded. A table that looks well stocked draws people in. Refill gaps as you sell.
  • Leave a clear spot at the front where people can put things down or pay.
  • Practise at home. Set up the whole table in your garden or kitchen the week before, and take a photo so you can copy it on the day.

Step 4: Set your prices and take payment

Pricing

Market prices need to cover more than your materials. Count your stall fee, travel, packaging and your time, too. It is tempting to price low because you are nervous, but low prices make it hard to keep going. Our guide on how to price your services shows a simple way to work out a fair price, and the same thinking works for products.

  • Use round, simple prices such as 5, 10 or 15, so paying is quick and you need less change.
  • Think about simple deals, such as "3 for 10", if they suit your products.
  • Look at what similar stalls charge, but do not try to be the cheapest. Be clearly worth your price.

Taking payment

Be ready for both cash and card. Many shoppers carry little cash these days.

  • A card reader. This is a small device that lets people pay by card or phone, usually linked to an app on your phone. Check the fees, set it up at home and do a test payment before the day. Our guide to taking card payments explains the choices.
  • A charged phone and a small portable charger, since the card reader often depends on it.
  • A cash float. This just means a supply of coins and small notes for giving change. Keep it in a cash box or a money belt, not loose on the table.
  • A written "card and cash welcome" sign, so people know they can pay either way.
  • Bags or wrapping for what people buy.

Are you counting the costs of getting started? Our plain-English guide to start-up costs helps you add up items like a gazebo, table, card reader and stall fees.

Step 5: Talk to shoppers without being pushy

You do not need to be a salesperson. Most shoppers at a local market like a friendly, relaxed stallholder who lets them look.

  • Stand up, smile and look up. Sitting behind the table looking at your phone tells people you are not open for business.
  • Say a simple hello when someone stops: "Hello, have a look, everything's made by me." Then give them space.
  • Answer questions warmly and share one or two interesting facts: where the fruit was grown, how long a quilt took, what the wood is.
  • Let people touch and taste where it is safe and allowed. Samples work well for food.
  • Do not chase people who walk away. Many come back later after they have looked around.
  • Hand out a card or leaflet with how to find you again, even to people who do not buy.

The same gentle approach works everywhere. Our guide to getting your first customers without being pushy has more ideas.

What to do on a slow day

Almost every stallholder has quiet days. Bad weather, a holiday weekend or a big event nearby can keep shoppers away. It is not a sign that your idea is wrong. Use the time well:

  • Tidy and refresh your table. Move things around and refill gaps.
  • Chat with neighbouring stallholders. They often know which markets are best, and may recommend you.
  • Collect contacts. Offer a simple sign-up sheet for people who want to hear about your next market. Only take details people choose to give.
  • Write down what you notice: what people looked at, what they asked, any comments on price.
  • Take a few photos of your stall for your website or social media, if you have them.

Judge a market over three or four visits, not one. If a slow start goes on for weeks, our guide on what to do when your new business feels slow helps you work out what to change.

After the market

When you get home, take ten minutes to write down:

  • What you sold, and what came home.
  • How much you took, and what the day cost you, including the stall fee and travel.
  • What people asked for that you did not have.
  • One or two things to change next time.

If a customer was especially happy, it is fine to ask them for a review. You can ask next time you see them, or give them a card with your review link. Our guide to getting your first customer reviews shows how to ask politely.

Your checklist for the night before and the morning

The night before

  • Stock packed, counted and labelled with prices
  • Table, tablecloth, crates or stands, and gazebo if you need one
  • Weights or ties for the gazebo, in case of wind
  • Sign with your business name
  • Card reader charged and tested, phone charged, portable charger
  • Cash float in a cash box or money belt
  • Bags or wrapping, business cards or leaflets, a pen and notebook
  • Any permits or insurance papers the organiser asked for
  • Check the weather and the set-up time

The morning

  • Warm layers, a waterproof, comfortable shoes and a folding chair
  • Water, a flask and snacks
  • Arrive early, so you can set up without rushing
  • Set up the table like your practice photo
  • Put your prices and "card and cash welcome" sign out
  • Take a deep breath, stand up, and smile

Your first market is a learning day as much as a selling day. Go gently, enjoy meeting people, and see what they tell you. Each market after that gets a little easier.
