Why one page is enough

A business plan has two possible jobs. The first is to persuade someone else to give you money: a bank, an investor or a grant programme. That kind of plan needs detail, forecasts and a particular format, because the reader is judging risk.

The second job is to help you think clearly. Before you spend savings, give up evenings or tell your family you are doing this, you want to be sure the idea holds together. Who will buy? What will they pay? What will it cost you? How will they hear about you? What happens if it goes slowly?

If you are starting a small service business, a side business or a modest shop with your own money, only the second job matters. And for that, one page is not a shortcut. It is often better than a long document, because:

  • You will finish it, and read it again. A single page can sit on your desk.
  • It forces clear decisions. There is no room to hide a vague idea behind paragraphs.
  • It is easy to change, and small businesses change a lot in their first year.

Our step-by-step guide to starting a business after 40 describes the plan as "one page: who your customers are, what you sell, what you charge, how people will find you and what it costs to run". This article shows you exactly how to write that page.

The nine sections of a one-page plan

Divide your page into nine short sections. Each one answers a single question in two or three lines. Write in plain sentences, as if explaining your business to a sensible friend: no jargon and no marketing language.

1. What I do

One sentence describing your business. A useful formula is: "I help [who] with [what] so they can [result]." For example: "I help busy homeowners keep their gardens tidy all year, so they can enjoy them without the work." If you can't write this sentence yet, that is the first thing to work on.

2. Who it is for

Describe your ideal customer as precisely as you can. Not "everyone", but something like "homeowners aged 55 and over in our town and the next two villages, who have medium or large gardens and would rather pay than do the work themselves." The more specific you are, the easier every other section becomes.

3. The problem I solve

Why would this person pay? What is annoying, difficult, time-consuming or worrying for them right now? Customers pay to solve problems, not to buy services. "Garden maintenance" is a service. "My garden is getting away from me and I can't manage the heavy work any more" is a problem.

4. What I sell and what I charge

List your main offers, ideally no more than three, with a price for each. For example: a one-off tidy-up, a monthly visit and a seasonal plan. Prices are where many new owners go wrong, so read how to price your services when you're just starting out before you fill this in. It shows you how to work out your minimum rate so you don't price below your real costs.

5. How customers will find me

Write down the two or three ways you will get customers in the first few months. Be concrete: "Tell 30 people I know personally", "Set up a Google Business Profile", "Put up cards in the two garden centres", "Ask every happy customer for a review". Our guide on how to get your first 10 customers has more ideas, and our Google Business Profile guide covers the most important free listing for a local business.

6. Why people will choose me

What makes you a better choice than the alternatives, which might be a competitor, a cheaper option, or the customer doing it themselves? For many people starting after 40, the honest answer is experience, reliability and trust: "Thirty years as a landscaper. I turn up when I say I will, and I'm fully insured." Avoid claims you can't back up.

7. The money

This is the section most worth spending time on. Write down three things:

  • Start-up costs: the one-off spending before your first sale, such as tools, insurance, a website, registration and any training.
  • Monthly running costs: what it costs to keep the business open each month, even with no customers, such as insurance, phone, software, fuel and website hosting.
  • Your break-even point: how many sales a month cover your running costs. The next section shows how to work it out.

Also write down your spending limit: the most you are willing to put in before the business pays for itself. Decide it now, while you are calm. If you are unsure what to include, see how much it really costs to start a small business.

8. My goals for the first 90 days

Three to five specific, measurable goals. Not "grow the business", but "Get my first five paying customers", "Collect three Google reviews" and "Earn enough to cover monthly costs by the end of month three". Ninety days is long enough to learn something real, and short enough to stay focused.

9. Risks and my safety limits

What could go wrong, and what will you do if it does? For example: "If I have fewer than three customers after three months, I'll change my offer or my prices before spending more." "I won't touch my retirement savings." "I'll keep my part-time job until the business covers its costs for six months in a row." This section protects you from the slow drift of spending a little more, then a little more.

Working out your break-even point

Break-even sounds technical, but it is simple division. You need two numbers:

  1. Your monthly running costs. Add up everything the business costs each month.
  2. What you keep from each sale. Take your price and subtract any costs that come with each sale, such as materials, fuel for that job or payment fees.

Then: break-even sales per month = monthly running costs ÷ what you keep from each sale.

A worked break-even example with made-up figures
ItemExample figure
Monthly running costs (insurance, phone, website, software)$300
Price of one garden visit$80
Cost of each visit (fuel, green waste, materials)$20
What you keep from each visit$60
Break-even: $300 ÷ $605 visits a month

These figures are made up to show the method; use your own. In this example, the first five visits each month just keep the doors open. Every visit after that is income for you, before tax. Knowing this number makes the business feel far less mysterious: you know exactly what "covering costs" means in real jobs.

Remember to pay yourself

Break-even means the business is not losing money. It does not mean it is paying you. Add a line for the income you want, and work out how many sales you need for that too. The pricing guide shows how.

An example one-page plan

Here is how a finished page might look. The business and the numbers are invented for illustration. They are not a real client.

What I do: I help homeowners keep their gardens tidy all year, so they can enjoy them without the heavy work.

Who it's for: Homeowners aged 55+ in town and two nearby villages with medium or large gardens, who would rather pay than do it themselves.

The problem: Their garden is getting away from them, the heavy jobs are getting harder, and it's hard to find someone reliable for small regular work.

What I sell: One-off tidy-up from $150. Monthly visit $80. Seasonal plan (four big visits a year) $280.

How they'll find me: Personal messages to 30 people I know. Google Business Profile. Cards at the two garden centres. A review request after every job.

Why me: Thirty years in landscaping. Insured. I turn up on time and leave the garden clean.

The money: Start-up about $1,200 (tools, insurance, website). Running costs about $300 a month. Break-even 5 monthly visits. Spending limit before profit: $2,000.

First 90 days: 5 paying customers. 3 Google reviews. Cover monthly costs by the end of month three.

Risks and limits: If fewer than 3 customers after 3 months, rethink the offer before spending more. Keep the part-time job until costs are covered for 6 months running. No borrowing.

That is the whole plan. It fits on one sheet, and every line can be checked against reality within a few months.

Test your plan before you trust it

A plan is a set of guesses. The fastest way to improve it is to test the riskiest guess first. Usually that is whether people will pay your price.

  • Talk to five to ten likely customers. Ask about their problem and what they pay now. Listen more than you pitch.
  • Make a few real sales at your planned price. Real money tells you more than polite encouragement.
  • Show the page to someone honest: an accountant, a mentor, or another small business owner. Ask them what looks unrealistic.

Then change the plan. Crossing things out means you are learning.

Keep it alive: the three-month review

Put a reminder in your calendar every three months. Sit down with the page and ask:

  • Did I hit my 90-day goals? Why, or why not?
  • Which customers were the best, and where did they come from?
  • Are my real costs higher or lower than I planned?
  • Am I charging enough? (If you are busy and nobody questions your price, probably not.)
  • Have any of my risks happened? Did I stick to my limits?
  • What are my goals for the next 90 days?

Write a fresh page each time and keep the old ones. Together they show how your business really grew.

When you do need a longer plan

There are times a one-page plan is not enough:

  • Applying for a bank loan. Lenders usually expect detailed financial projections, your experience and how you will repay.
  • Seeking investors or partners who are putting money in.
  • Some grants and government programmes, which often have their own required format.

Even then, your one-page plan is the best starting point: each of the nine sections simply becomes a longer chapter. Free help is available. In the US, the Small Business Administration publishes guidance on writing business plans, and SCORE offers free templates and mentoring. Most countries have similar government-backed small business services. An accountant can help with the financial projections.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Waiting for perfect. A rough page this week beats a polished document next year.
  • Skipping the money section. It is the part people avoid and the part that matters most.
  • Hopeful numbers. Use real quotes and cautious guesses.
  • No limits. Without a spending limit, it is easy to keep putting money in.
  • Never looking again. The value is in the three-month review.
