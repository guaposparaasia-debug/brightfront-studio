Why new businesses are targeted

When you register a business name, form a company, file a trademark or buy a domain, some of your details often appear in public records. Scammers collect this information and send letters and emails that look like official follow-ups. New owners are busy, still learning which bills are real, and keen to do everything correctly. That combination is exactly what scammers rely on.

The good news is that most scams follow a handful of patterns. Here are the ten you are most likely to see.

1. Fake domain renewal notices

A letter or email warns that your domain name (like yourbusiness.com) is about to expire and must be renewed. It looks like an invoice. In reality, it comes from a different company trying to transfer your domain to them at a much higher price, or simply take your money.

How to spot it: it does not come from the company you bought your domain from. What to do: log in to your real domain provider's account to check the expiry date. Turning on auto-renew there removes the worry.

2. Fake trademark and registration letters

After filing a trademark or registering a business, you may receive official-looking letters offering to "register" or "monitor" your trademark, add you to a "business register", or provide a required "certificate". They often use government-style names, seals and reference numbers, but are private companies charging large fees for things you don't need. Trademark offices, including the US Patent and Trademark Office, publish warnings about these.

What to do: check any request against the official government website, contacting the office using details you find yourself, not those in the letter.

3. "Annual compliance" mailers

Similar to the above: letters that mimic state or government agencies, saying you must file an annual report, buy a "certificate of good standing" or pay a compliance fee, often for several times the real cost or for something not required at all.

What to do: find out the real requirements and fees for your business type on your government's official website. Ask your accountant if unsure.

4. Google listing and "SEO" calls

Automated or live calls claim to be "from Google" or "working with Google", warning that your listing will be removed unless you pay, or promising a guaranteed first-place ranking for a fee.

The facts: creating and verifying a Google Business Profile is free. Nobody can guarantee a #1 position in Google's results. What to do: hang up and manage your profile yourself. Our Google Business Profile guide shows you how.

5. Fake directory and advertising invoices

You receive an invoice for an advertisement or directory listing you never ordered, or a "proof" of an ad that becomes a contract if you sign or don't reply. Some callers claim you agreed on a previous call and may even have a recording edited to sound like you said "yes".

What to do: only pay invoices for things you knowingly ordered. Keep a simple list of your real suppliers. Don't say "yes" to a caller you don't know; ask for everything in writing.

6. Changed bank details (invoice fraud)

This is one of the most costly scams for small businesses. You receive an email that appears to be from a real supplier, landlord, builder or customer, saying their bank details have changed and the next payment should go to a new account. Sometimes the scammer has actually hacked the real person's email, so the message looks completely genuine.

What to do: never change payment details based on an email alone. Call the person on a phone number you already have, not one in the email, and confirm. Make this a firm rule for everyone in your business.

7. Overpayment scams

A new "customer" books a large order or job and pays more than the price, often by cheque or a payment that later turns out to be fake. They ask you to refund the difference or send money to a "courier" or "supplier". When the original payment bounces, you have lost the refund.

What to do: be wary of customers who overpay and ask for money back. Wait until payments have fully cleared, and never pay third parties on a customer's behalf.

8. Impostor calls from "tax" or "government" officials

A caller says they are from the tax office or another agency, that you owe money, and that you will be fined or arrested unless you pay immediately, often by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency.

The facts: real tax authorities do not demand immediate payment by gift card or cryptocurrency, and do not threaten arrest over the phone. What to do: hang up and call the agency's official number from its website.

9. Tech support and "your website is hacked" scams

A pop-up, call or email says your computer or website has a virus or security problem and asks you to call a number, install software, or give someone remote access. They then "fix" the fake problem for a fee, or steal information.

What to do: never let someone you don't know access your computer remotely. If you are worried about your website, contact the company that built or hosts it directly.

10. Phishing emails and fake logins

Emails that look like they come from your bank, a payment provider, a delivery company or a software service, asking you to "confirm your account" or "fix a payment problem" by clicking a link. The link goes to a fake login page that steals your password. With AI tools, these emails are now better written than ever, so spelling mistakes are no longer a reliable warning sign.

What to do: don't click links in unexpected emails. Go to the website by typing the address yourself, or use the official app. Turn on two-step verification (a code sent to your phone) for your email, bank and important accounts.

The warning signs, all in one place

  • Urgency: "Pay today or lose your domain, listing or registration."
  • Unusual payment methods: gift cards, wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or a new bank account.
  • Official-looking but unfamiliar: government-style names, seals and reference numbers from an organisation you didn't deal with.
  • Unsolicited contact: they contacted you, not the other way round.
  • Guarantees: "#1 on Google", "guaranteed customers".
  • Requests for secrecy or remote access.
  • Pressure to say "yes" on a call before you've seen anything in writing.

Simple habits that protect your business

  1. Keep a list of your real suppliers: domain provider, website host, accountant, insurer, software. If a bill comes from anyone else, be suspicious.
  2. Turn on auto-renew for your domain with your real provider.
  3. Use two-step verification on email, banking and payment accounts.
  4. Call to confirm any change of bank details, using a number you already have.
  5. Pause before paying. A genuine bill can wait a day while you check. Scammers hate delay.
  6. Ask someone. Your accountant, your web designer or a trusted fellow business owner can often tell in seconds whether something is real.
Already caught?

Call your bank or card company straight away, since quick action can sometimes stop a payment. Change any passwords you shared. Keep the letters and emails as evidence, and report the scam: in the US at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, in the UK to Action Fraud, in Australia to Scamwatch and in Canada to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre. Being scammed is nothing to be embarrassed about; these schemes are designed by professionals to fool careful people.

Most scams fall apart the moment you slow down and check. Make "pause and verify" a habit, and you will be safe from the vast majority of them.
